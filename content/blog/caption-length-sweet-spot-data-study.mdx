Caption advice has gone in circles for years: keep it short, no, write an essay for SEO, no, just hashtags, no, no hashtags. We measured all of it on one dataset.

We pulled **5,030 short-form videos** from **76 creators** in The Content Labs dataset, read every caption for length, hashtag count, question marks and emoji, and compared each video against that same creator's normal views. Measuring against the creator's own median is what makes a big account and a small account comparable; a 1.0x always means "a normal video for this person."

<Highlight title="Medium caption, zero hashtags">
Captions of **150 to 300 characters** ran at **1.13x** the creator's normal views across 2,810 videos, with **35.0%** doubling and **17.2%** doing five times it. Captions **under 50 characters** ran at **0.96x** with only 20.1% doubling. And videos with **no hashtags** ran at **1.08x**, ahead of one to three hashtags (**0.97x**) and four to eight (**1.02x**).
</Highlight>

The shape is a hill with the top at 150 to 300 characters. Short captions (under 50, 338 videos) and shortish captions (50 to 150, 817 videos) both ran at 0.96x. The 150 to 300 band is the largest (2,810 videos from 28 creators) and the strongest. Past 300 characters it drops to 1.04x across 1,065 videos.

Two to four sentences, in other words. Enough to state the claim, add the one detail that makes it specific and give the feed something to index. Not a paragraph.

The under-50 bucket is worth a second look. It has the lowest breakout rate in this study (20.1%), the lowest 5x rate (7.7%) and average views of 8,453. A one-word caption with a hashtag is what a throwaway video looks like, and the numbers treat it that way.

No hashtags was the best bucket, and it was not close to being a small sample: 3,662 videos from 21 creators ran at 1.08x, doubled 33.2% of the time and did 5x 15.8% of the time. One to three hashtags, the "just a couple of relevant ones" advice, ran at 0.97x across 991 videos. Four to eight ran at 1.02x across 376 videos, with average views of 27,375 against 138,959 for the no-hashtag bucket.

This does not prove hashtags lower reach. It proves the creators who stopped using them did not pay for it, which is the question most creators are actually asking.

<Callout title="The practical rule">
Write the claim as a sentence, add the one detail that makes it specific, and stop between 150 and 300 characters. No question mark. No hashtags unless you have a reason that is not "reach." An emoji is fine if it is how you write.
</Callout>

## Caveats

The creators in this dataset skew toward commentary, sports and news, where captions carry a claim rather than a product name or a recipe. A niche where hashtags still function as search (some Instagram categories) may differ. The direction on caption length and on question marks is the part we would bet on anywhere.

## How we ran this

Every video was processed by The Content Labs analysis engine. Caption length, hashtag count, question marks and emoji are read straight from the posted caption, with no model involved. We then divided each video's views by that creator's median views over the period, so the numbers are relative to each creator's normal rather than raw counts. Buckets with fewer than 60 videos are not reported. The dataset is 9,289 analysed videos from 76 creators across TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, Facebook and X; 5,030 had a caption and a measured baseline.

## FAQ

**How big was the dataset?**
5,030 short-form videos with a caption, from 76 creators on The Content Labs, drawn from 9,289 analysed videos across five platforms.

**Is 150 to 300 characters words or letters?**
Characters, including spaces. Roughly 25 to 50 words.

**Why measure against the creator's normal video instead of raw views?**
Raw views mostly measure account size. Dividing by the creator's own median isolates what the caption contributed.

**Do hashtags hurt?**
The data cannot say they hurt. It says that dropping them did not cost the creators who dropped them anything, and their videos ran at 1.08x.
